Lesson 9: Approaches to Literary Criticism

Approaches in Literary Criticism

  • Formalism
  • Postmodern
  • Marxism
  • Feminism
  • Mimetic
  • Psychoanalytic
  • Moral-Philosophical
  • Reader-Response

Formalism

Description: The default approach involving the close analysis of formal elements within the text.

Key Elements Examined:

  • Characters, Settings, Plot, and Theme
  • Point of View (POV) and Persona
  • Dramatic Situation and Figurative Language

Guiding Questions:

  • Who are the characters in the story?
  • Where did the story take place?
  • What is the story all about?
  • How did the author narrate the story? What POV did the author use?

Postmodern

Description: Rejects grand narratives and universal truths, embraces fragmentation and ambiguity, breaks traditional literary forms, focuses on intertextuality, and celebrates the artificial and self-referential.

Examples & Applications:

  • Breaks fairy tale rules (e.g., Shrek doesn't follow the usual "handsome prince saves princess" story).
  • Shows multiple truths where not all characters are simply "good" or "bad" (e.g., the ogre is kind while the prince, Lord Farquaad, is selfish).

Marxism

Description: Based on a social philosophy established by Karl Marx and Friedrich Engels during the 19th century. Implies that those who have money have power, while those who lack wealth experience oppression. Examines social stratification and class struggles between the upper class (bourgeoisie) and working class (proletariat).

Examples & Applications:

  • The story focuses on the unfair system in Victorian England, where the rich (nobles) have power and the poor suffer.
  • Moriarty fights the rich system; criticizes the elite and gives voice to the oppressed.

Feminism

Description: Looks into the roles of women in a given text through gender ideologies, promoted stereotypes, character interactions, and societal implications.

Examples & Applications:

  • Focuses on a strong female lead; shows women as strong and capable rather than weak or secondary.
  • Challenges gender roles (e.g., Enola chooses her own path in a time when girls were expected to marry and behave "properly", breaking rules made by a male-dominated society and highlighting how society limits women).

Mimetic

Description: Originating with Plato, who believed all art is an imitation (mimesis) of reality. Examines how literary works mirror reality, portray truth, show human nature through realistic problems and feelings, and reveal lessons about society, ethics, and human behavior.

Examples & Applications:

  • Shows real problems in society, reflecting true issues like poverty, crime, and corruption.
  • Settings like Payatas (a real garbage dump site in Manila) expose how the poor live and how the justice system can fail them, helping readers understand real-world issues.

Psychoanalytic

Description: Inspired by Sigmund Freud. Focuses on the unconscious mind, hidden motivations (fear, guilt, repressed desires), and the components of the mind: Id (desires), Ego (reality), and Superego (morality). Aims to uncover the author's reasons for writing and factors driving character behavior.

Examples & Applications:

  • Shows repressed trauma and emotional wounds (e.g., characters like Moon Gang-tae and Ko Moon-young carrying childhood trauma that affects adult behavior).
  • Analyzes how past pain shapes present actions using symbols like castles and storybooks representing emotional pain and the need for healing.

Moral-Philosophical

Description: Focuses on the ethical and moral judgments of characters and authors, emphasizing values, ethics, and lessons. Asks what stories teach about right and wrong, expecting writers to uplift society and readers to reflect on moral impacts.

Examples & Applications:

  • The Sybil System decides who is "dangerous" before committing a crime, prompting questions on whether it is morally right to punish someone for an uncommitted crime.
  • Characters like Akane question system fairness, warning that a society without compassion or freedom is not truly just (e.g., Psycho-Pass encouraging reflection on power, freedom, morality, and justice).

Reader-Response

Description: Examines literary works based on how readers interpret or understand meaning and context. Focuses on the reader's active personal experience, recognizing that meaning is created through personal response rather than residing solely in the text, leading different readers to derive unique meanings based on their backgrounds and emotions.
