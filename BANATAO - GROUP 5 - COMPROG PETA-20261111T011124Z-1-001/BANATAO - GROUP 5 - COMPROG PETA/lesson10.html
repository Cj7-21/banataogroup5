Lesson 10: Philippine Literature

What is Philippine Literature?

Description: It is a product, a reflection of and reaction to the period, place and people who produced it. (Balabar, et al., 1989, p.5) that Filipinos, whether native, naturalized, or foreign-born, have created about the experience of people living in or relating to Philippine Society

Reasons for Studying Philippine Literature:

  • To appreciate our literary heritage.
  • To realize our literary limitations conditioned by certain historical factors so we can take steps to overcome them.
  • To understand that we have a great and noble tradition which can serve as a means to assimilate other cultures.
  • To show that as Filipinos who truly love and take pride in our own culture, we have to manifest our own literature.

Pre-Colonial Period (BC to 1564)

Description: The longest period in Philippine literature. It was predominantly oral literature passed on from generation to generation, marked by our ancestors and depicting daily life, explanations for natural phenomena, and the beginnings of ritual.

Baybayin is a pre-colonial Philippine syllabic script (an abugida), used for writing various Philippine languages. The term “baybayin” comes from the Tagalog root baybay meaning “to spell” or “to syllabicate.” Baybayin letters are curved and flowing, making them easy to carve on bamboo, palm leaves, and wood.

Literary Forms & Examples:

  • Proverbs (Salawikain): Practical observations and philosophies of everyday life that are usually written in a rhyming scheme. Examples: "Kung ano ang puno s'ya ang bunga" and "Ang hindi marunong lumingon sa pinanggalingan ay hindi makakarating sa paroroonan".
  • Folk Songs: Beautiful songs that are informal expressions of our ancestors’ experiences in life covering Courtship, Lullabies, Harvests, and Funerals.
  • Tales: Stories of origin or certain places, their names, and their creation, also known as myths and legends. Examples: Alamat ng Pinya, Alamat ng Bundok Makiling.
  • Epics: Long-winded poems about a hero and his adventures and misadventures, featuring a male hero with pleasing qualities and superhuman capabilities. Examples: Hinilawod, Biag ni Lam-Ang.

Spanish Period (1565–1898)

Description: Began when Miguel López de Legazpi was appointed as the first Spanish governor-general in 1565, establishing Cebu and later moving the capital to Manila in 1571. Spanish missionaries introduced Christianity, replaced the first Filipino alphabet (Alibata/Baybayin) with the Roman alphabet, taught the Christian Doctrine, and used Spanish to record laws, ordinances, and colonial policies while lending many words to local languages.

Early Written Forms:

  • Doctrinas: Religious texts, catechisms, and prayers translated into local languages using both Latin and Baybayin scripts.
  • Pasyon and Devotional Literature: Blended Spanish Catholic teachings with Filipino oral traditions.

Recreational Plays & Literary Forms:

  • Tibag: Ritual brought by Spaniards to remind people about St. Helena's search for the Cross on which Jesus died.
  • Lagaylay & Cenakulo: Special Maytime social gathering in Sorsogon and a dramatic performance to commemorate the passion and death of Jesus Christ.
  • Panunuluyan & Salubong: Christmas Eve presentation before 12:00 and Easter play dramatizing the meeting of the Risen Christ and His Mother.
  • Carillo, Zarzuela, & Sainete: Shadow play on a moonless night, the father of drama, and 18th-century short musical comedies.
  • Moro-Moro, Karagatan, & Duplo: Town fiesta entertainment for Christian religion, socio-religious death vigil, and poetic jousts.
  • Balagtasan, Dung-aw, Awit, and Corrido: Debate contests in verse, bereavement chants in free verse, and metrical romances.

Revolutionary & Propaganda Period

Description: Triggered by the execution of Gomburza (Mariano Gómez, José Burgos, and Jacinto Zamora) in 1872 due to false accusations in the Cavite Mutiny, which awakened Filipino nationalism. It featured peaceful reform movements (Propaganda Literature led by Rizal, M.H. del Pilar, and Graciano López Jaena via La Solidaridad) and violent revolutionary movements demanding complete independence through the Katipunan (founded by Andrés Bonifacio in 1892) leading up to the Philippine Revolution (1896–1898).

Propaganda Literature - Reformatory in objective: A peaceful reform movement led by educated Filipinos (like Jose Rizal, Marcelo H. del Pilar, and Graciano López Jaena) using newspapers (La Solidaridad) and political essays to call for reforms, equal rights, and representation in Spain.

Key Works, Masterpieces & Figures:

  • Noli Me Tangere (1887) & El Filibusterismo (1891): José Rizal's political novels exposing Spanish friar abuses and colonial corruption. Noli Me Tangere is a romantic yet socially critical novel while El Filibusterismo is a darker sequel advocating for revolution, dedicated to Gomburza.
  • Revolutionary Essays & Poetry: Kalayaan (newspaper edited by Emilio Jacinto), True Decalogue (Apolinario Mabini), Katapusang Hibik ng Pilipinas (Andres Bonifacio), and Liwanag at Dilim (Emilio Jacinto).
  • Historical Milestones: Execution of José Rizal on December 30, 1896, and the Declaration of Independence by Emilio Aguinaldo in Kawit, Cavite on June 12, 1898.

American Period (1898–1941)

Description: Initiated by the Treaty of Paris on December 10, 1898 (where Spain ceded the Philippines to the U.S. for $20 million), ignoring prior Filipino independence declarations and causing the Philippine-American War (1899–1902). Americans introduced religious freedom, free public education systems, the Thomasites (arrived in 1901 aboard the USS Thomas), and English as the official medium of instruction, uniting diverse native dialects and connecting the Philippines to the Western world.

Evolution of Writing & Apprenticeship:

  • Early writers copied Western styles (Romanticism and Realism), often struggling with English grammar, diction, and rhythm, resulting in overly sentimental and exaggerated poetry.
  • Period of Apprenticeship (1910–1930) Short Stories: “Dead Stars” by Paz Marquez Benitez, “The Key” by Paz Latorena, and “Footnote to Youth” by Jose Garcia Villa.
  • Period of Emergence (1920–1930): Marked by Jose Garcia Villa ("Doveglion" and "Poet of the Century"), famous for introducing comma poetry and modernist techniques. Poem Excerpt: "In my Desire to be Nude / I clothed myself in fire..."

Japanese Period & War Years (1941–1945)

Description: Triggered by the Pearl Harbor attack on December 7, 1941, followed by air raids on Philippine military bases on December 8, 1941. This led to the invasion, the Fall of Bataan, and the brutal Bataan Death March in April 1942. The Japanese military promoted Tagalog and local languages over English under the "Asia for Asians" propaganda, shifting literary focus toward national pride, patriotism, emotional realism, and wartime hardships.

Literary Forms and Milestones:

  • Poetry shifted away from Francisco Balagtas's ornate style toward simpler language, free verse, haiku, and tanaga.
  • Fiction rose over poetry, emphasizing realistic storytelling and ordinary Filipino resilience.
  • 25 Pinakamabuting Maikling Kathang Pilipino (1943): A compilation of Tagalog short stories organized by the Japanese military government to reinforce cultural identity separate from Western influence.
  • Drama featured simple storylines reflecting Filipino traditions and moral values under occupation.

Period of Maturity and Originality (1945–1960) & Literary Giants

Description: Post-war writers developed original voices using both English and Filipino. Literature explored social realities, post-war experiences, exile, identity, and philosophical introspection, moving further away from Western imitation.

Nick Joaquin (1917–2004) - National Artist for Literature known for exploring Philippine identity, history, and culture through symbolism and magical realism.

Notable Literary Giants & Figures:

  • Jose Garcia Villa: "Doveglion" and "Poet of the Century".
  • N.V.M. Gonzalez & Bienvenido Santos: Explored rural life, resilience, exile, nostalgia, and immigrant experiences.
  • Gregorio Brillantes & Gilda Cordero-Fernando: Blended realism with introspection, humor, and portrayals of family life.
  • Nick Joaquin (1917–2004): National Artist for Literature who blended history, myth, religion, symbolism, and magical realism to explore Philippine identity under the pen name Quijano de Manila.
    Notable Works: “The Woman Who Had Two Navels” (1961), “A Portrait of the Artist as Filipino” (1950), “May Day Eve”, and “Summer Solstice”.
